The old lighthouse stood at the edge of the cliff, and every evening the keeper climbed its narrow stairs to light the lamp. His name was Tomas, and he had kept the light burning for thirty years without missing a single night.

"Why do you climb all those stairs every day?" asked a boy who visited him one summer.

Tomas smiled and looked out at the dark water. "Because somewhere out there," he said, "a small boat is looking for this light. If I forget, even for one night, someone might lose their way home."

The boy thought about this for a long time. He watched the waves crash against the rocks below, white and loud, and then fade back into the grey sea.

"What if nobody is out there tonight?" the boy asked.

"Then I light it anyway," Tomas said. "Because I cannot know for certain, and the one night I guess wrong is the night someone needed it most."

Every morning, before the sun rose, Tomas walked down to the shore and checked the small wooden boats tied along the dock. He counted them one by one: the red boat, the blue boat, the little green boat with a broken oar, and the old grey one that nobody used anymore.

Some days the sea was calm, and the boats barely moved. Other days, storms rolled in from the west, and the wind howled through the lighthouse windows like a hundred voices. On those nights, Tomas stayed awake, watching the lamp, listening for the sound of a horn or a bell that would mean a ship was in trouble.

One winter night, the storm came faster than anyone expected. Rain hammered the windows, and the sea rose higher than Tomas had ever seen. He lit the lamp early and stood by the glass, watching the darkness for any sign of movement.

Hours passed. The wind screamed. Then, just past midnight, he saw it: a small light bobbing far out on the water, rising and falling with the waves.

Tomas ran down the stairs two at a time, grabbed a lantern and a rope, and hurried to the dock. He untied the old grey boat, the one nobody used, and pushed it into the churning sea.

"Are you mad?" shouted a neighbor from the shore. "Nobody sails in weather like this!"

"Someone is already out there," Tomas called back. "I am not sailing into the storm. I am going to bring them home."

He rowed hard against the waves, his arms burning, salt water stinging his eyes. The little light ahead flickered, disappeared, then flickered again. Finally, he reached it: a small fishing boat, half filled with water, and inside it a young fisherman named Elin, soaked and shivering, clinging to the mast.

"I thought no one would come," Elin said through chattering teeth.

"The light was still burning," Tomas answered. "I could not let it burn for nothing."

Together they rowed back through the storm, guided by the steady beam from the lighthouse above. By the time they reached the shore, the rain had softened, and the first grey light of dawn was creeping over the horizon.

After that night, Elin often visited the lighthouse. She asked Tomas to teach her how the great lamp worked, how the oil was measured, how the glass was cleaned, and how the light was aimed so far out across the water.

"Someday," she told him, "I want to keep a light like this myself."

Tomas nodded slowly. "Then you must promise something first."

"What is that?"

"Promise that even on the nights when you are certain no one needs the light, you will climb the stairs and light it anyway."

Elin thought about the storm, about the boat filling with water, about how close she had come to being lost in the dark. "I promise," she said.

Years later, when Tomas grew too old to climb the stairs, it was Elin who lit the lamp each evening. The villagers said the light never went dark, not even once, not even during the worst storms of winter.

And on quiet nights, when the sea was calm and the sky was full of stars, Elin would sit by the window and watch the water, thinking of the boy who once asked why anyone would climb so many stairs for a light that might shine on nothing at all.

She always answered the same way Tomas had. Because somewhere out there, someone might be looking for it.
